# Blueprint drawing rules

A person should understand and judge an architecture from `.abbd/blueprint/index.html` quickly. What makes a blueprint hard to read is not the number of relations. It is lines that run the same way but are drawn apart, lines that run against the eye or out of their way, and views that try to show too much at once. The bar is a diagram a person polished by hand in a tool like draw.io, not a dump of extracted edges. So the blueprint follows three principles, and every rule below comes from one of them.

- **Decomposition.** The blueprint is a tree of views, all drawn the same way. The overview shows the top level. Every unit that holds others has a view of its children, with that unit's contracts on the frame. A box that holds units opens its view. Each view stays small because the state is decomposed, never because relations are left out.
- **Convergence.** Lines that come from one place travel together as one trunk and branch only near their ends. Lines that go to one place, or to boxes side by side that the same places reach, merge midway into one trunk and split only in the gap above them. Every trunk carries a count. A reader follows one trunk instead of a braid.
- **Flow.** A drawing reads top to bottom and left to right. What calls or uses sits above and to the left. What it calls or uses sits below and to the right. Lines point down or right, and run on the grid in the spaces between boxes, the short way. The few that must climb are drawn dashed.

`internal/screen/legible_test.go` checks every rule on the rendered SVG, which is what a reader sees. `internal/screen/trace_test.go` traces every relation along the drawn ink, the way a reader follows a line, for rules 16 to 23. `internal/screen/fixtures_test.go` applies the checks to every view of four real states: this repository, ripgrep, Orca and pstack's skill tree. The rule numbers below match the checker's messages.

## Decomposition

1. **At most 16 boxes per view.** Top-level contracts don't count, because they are drawn as chips, and neither does a focus view's twin. A level that holds more is split only along a grouping the project shows (see Grouping levels). Otherwise it stays as it is, a debt of this rule, and its focus views read it one box at a time.
2. **No relation is dropped.** Every live relation between present units appears in some view. It can be a line, a bundle, a contract chip, the list of a reader's couplings under a module's inside, or the list of relations between two contracts. Each wire and chip records the stored relations it stands for, and the test compares those records with the state.
3. **Width budget.** The overview is at most 1320px wide and a unit's view at most 920px. That is what a 1440px screen shows of each. A layer that is too wide wraps into rows. A module on the overview whose chips need more than 480px wraps them into up to two rows inside its box instead of widening, so lines can pass beside it; the last row folds what still does not fit into one `+N` chip that names them all. A frame never grows past the budget: chips that do not fit on their side move to the other side, and those that fit on neither fold into one `+N` chip. On a 390px screen the drawing scrolls inside its box and the page itself never scrolls sideways.

## Focus views

Some views stay a tangle however they are laid out: their boxes use each other many times over. Orca's desktop main process holds 15 units and draws 78 relations, and the median relation crosses 9 others. pstack's twelve families use each other in 42 of 66 pairs. A reader cannot follow one box's lines through all the others. So such a view is also read one box at a time, through views derived from it (`focus.go`).

A view is dense when it draws more than two relations per box on average. Each box of a dense view then gets a focus view. A focus view of box X in view V is a partial graph of V:

- **Selected.** X and the boxes of V that X uses or that use X: the ring.
- **Drawn.** Only the lines with an end at X. A line between two ring boxes is left out here and drawn in the focus view of either of them.
- **Lifted.** X's lines to anything outside V's scope stay on the frame, lifted to the largest unit that holds their end, exactly as in V. On the overview, a contract X provides brings the modules that use it, and a contract X uses brings the modules that provide it.
- **Collapsed.** Nothing new. A ring box that holds units stays one box, as in V.
- **Placed.** Callers above X, what X uses below it, and X at the middle of the drawing. The lines from the frame to X run down the same middle, so they drop straight into it, and each row of the ring packs around them. A row that cannot reach that far without passing the width budget moves in only as far as it must.
- **Mental map.** The boxes of each ring row stand left to right in the order they stand in V, so a box the reader found left of another in V is still left of it here. A ring too wide for one row splits into rows that each take every k-th box: each row still runs across V from left to right, and a box of one row stands over a gap in the next, where its line passes down. A test holds the order and the centred focus across the fixtures.
- **Twins.** A ring box that both uses X and is used by it is drawn twice, once on each side, so every line of the view reads down and no line between the pair runs both ways through a bundle. The second box is a twin: a unit of the drawing only. Its tooltip and its sub-line say it is also drawn above, and rule 1 counts it once.
- **Width.** A focus view stands on its own page section with no text beside it, so it takes the overview's width budget, and its unit boxes show only the last segment of a path-like name, as frame ports do.
- **Back to the stored graph.** Every line keeps the stored relations it stands for, as in V. Each ring box opens its own focus view, X opens its inside when it has one, and the section links back to V.

A focus view stores nothing: it is cut from V's own lines at render time, never found again, so it draws no line, chip or box that V lacks. V stays as it is, and every relation V draws has a box at one end, so it is drawn in that box's focus view too. A test holds both directions across the fixtures. Under a dense view the page lists every focus view as a row of links, and a box of V that opens nothing else opens its focus view. The graph file leaves focus views and the links to them out.

A focus view never groups, and neither does any other view. The renderer draws no box the state lacks, so a cycle is drawn in the view of the unit that really holds it: its units keep their own boxes, the lines that close it climb dashed under rule 13, and each of its boxes is read on its own through its focus view.

## Convergence

4. **One bundle per place and direction.** Lines of one kind that leave one edge of a box share a departure bus: one stub out of the box, then one trunk down through every row at least two of its lines still pass, heading row by row toward the places they reach. A line leaves the trunk in the gap above the first row it would pass alone. Lines of one kind that reach one edge of a box share an arrival bus: one trunk through every row they pass, one track in the gap above the box, and a drop into each chip. A line joins that trunk once it is off its departure trunk, so lines from different places merge midway and split only at the end; a line that rides its departure trunk into its last gap steps onto the arrival track where the trunk crosses that gap. Boxes side by side in one row that are reached from exactly the same places share one arrival bus that splits only in the gap above them. Boxes reached from places that only overlap get a bus each: a shared trunk would draw a path from every one of its places to every box it splits to (rule 18). Lines that all ride one departure bus, one into each box, get no second bus: they split off the departure track straight into their boxes. Frame ports folded into one `+N` chip are one place, so their lines share the chip's slots and buses. Each drop shows how many of the bus's lines reach that box as `×N`. The checker counts lines that leave or reach a box edge beside the bundle of their kind, and expects 0 of each.
5. **At most 4 lines per channel.** Vertical lines closer than 12px count as one channel. A fifth line takes a lane past a wider gap.
6. **Parallel lines stay apart.** Two lines never run closer than 5px to each other for more than 24px, except within 26px of a shared end.
7. **At most 2 crossings per drawn line, counted after bundling.** A drawn line is counted where it arrives: a bus is one line however many pieces draw it. Lines run on a grid, so every crossing is at a right angle.
14. **No braids.** Lines of different bundles may run side by side within one channel for at most 200px per drawn line, summed over every pair, away from their ends. Lines that run that far together belong on one trunk.

The lines that leave one edge of a box and the lines that reach it share that edge's slots, so a line arriving at a box's bottom never lands on a line leaving it there. A line that passes a row never takes the x of a line passing the row next to it, since the two would meet in the gap between the rows and read as one. A line's stub, or its drop, keeps clear of the chains passing the row next to its box edge for the same reason.

Each trunk runs down the chain of one of its lines, its lead. A departure trunk takes the chain of its deepest line nearest the box. An arrival trunk takes the chain of the line that joins it first. The bus's stub or drop stands right over that chain, so the trunk leaves or enters its box without a bend. Only one departure track may meet an arrival trunk, at the trunk's top. Any other line that leaves a departure trunk for that arrival trunk steps down off its departure track onto a short track of its own, which ends on the trunk. That step stands between the two trunks and never past the departure trunk, where its track would hook back. It takes the first x no passing line and no other step uses, two lanes or more off the arrival trunk, then one lane off. Where the trunks stand too close for that, it stands halfway between them. A line that rides its departure trunk into its last gap steps down onto its arrival track at an x that no other step and no passing line uses. It steps down where the trunk ends when it can, and above its own end when the trunk runs on below. Where every x from there to its end is taken, it takes the nearest free x past its end or behind the trunk, up to 32 lanes out. Only when none of those is free does it step where it would first have, on a line already there. Each of these keeps a reader on the trunk from turning onto ink that leads to places the trunk's lines never reach (rule 18).

Two modules on the overview that depend on each other directly are joined by one line with a head at each end, drawn the way the eye reads, instead of two lines of which one would climb. Its tooltip names both directions and it stands for both relations.

## Legibility

8. **Labels are whole.** A chip is never cut: it is as wide as its label up to 220px, and a longer label wraps onto more lines, so the chip stays inside the edge it sits on and inside the canvas. A name wraps to two lines and widens its box. It is cut only past the hard width (280px for a module box, 220px for a unit box), and then the tooltip carries the full name. No label is wider than its box.
9. **Nothing overlaps.** No two boxes or chips overlap, and no line passes through a box it does not join.
10. **A port sits on the side its lines flow from.** On a unit's frame, a contract the unit provides and a caller from outside sit on the top edge. What the unit uses sits on the bottom edge, even a contract it also provides. While the view is layered, the frame's top and bottom take part as two extra nodes, so a unit that meets a port on top rises toward it and one that uses a port below sinks toward it. A filled chip is a contract the unit provides and an outlined chip is one it uses.
11. **A line's colour shows its kind, not its origin.** A line a pstack reader found (판독) starts with a hollow purple dot. A line a human stated (확인) starts with a filled green dot. A line with no dot was extracted from the code (추출). The dot's tooltip says what the word means. 판독 means a reader read the relation, ABBD checks on every sync that the quoted code still exists, and no human has confirmed it.
12. **Unwired contracts are grouped.** A contract that no inside unit is recorded as meeting is not spread along the frame. It is listed under the drawing as a chip group, with a shared first word shown once (`abbd`: diff, evidence, ...).

## Flow

13. **Lines read down and right.** The checker takes every line where it arrives at a box or a chip. The line runs forward when its end lies below its start, or level with it and to the right. A line that provides a contract reads from the contract to the unit that meets it, so it counts the other way round. A line that runs back, up or left, must be dashed, and the checker expects 0 back lines drawn solid. At most one line, or 20% of the arriving lines if that is more, may run back.
15. **Lines take the short way.** No line runs more than 240px longer than the shortest grid path between its ends. A line that passes rows runs down its chain (see below). A line to a frame chip whose chain lies more than 160px off the way between its ends passes the rows on its own instead, since the chips are set out after the layout. A line with no chain takes a lane in the space between two boxes, at least 13px from any chain, or passes beside the row straight down where it is headed, a lane outward from any line already there.

The layout keeps rule 13 by layering what uses what: a unit sits above everything it uses, and a cycle's edges are reversed for the layering only (Eades, Lin and Smyth), so the fewest lines climb.

The rows are then laid out as a proper layered graph, the way Sugiyama, dot and ELK do (`layered.go`). The data shape is rows of items, where an item is a box or a dummy. Each pair of places more than one row apart gets a chain: one dummy in every row between them, as wide as the lanes its lines need. A frame's top and bottom take part as a row above the first and a row below the last.

- **Ordering.** Each row is sorted by the weighted mean position of its neighbours in the row above, then below, and adjacent items swap while that crosses less. The order with the fewest crossings over all adjacent rows, dummies included, is kept. A layer too wide for the budget wraps into rows in that order, and the rows are ordered again with the chains the wrapping adds.
- **Coordinates.** Each box is a block and so is each chain, as in Brandes and Köpf, so a chain is placed as one vertical line and a box steps aside where a chain must pass. Two chains that cross cannot both be straight, so the later one stays one block per dummy. The blocks start halfway between their leftmost and rightmost places. Each then moves, within what its neighbours leave it, toward the weighted median of what it is joined to, with weights 1, 2 and 8 for box to box, box to dummy and dummy to dummy, as in dot. A chain moves toward an x it can drop straight from at one of its ends. A drawing still wider than its budget gives up its shortest chains.
- **Chips.** A chip on a frame's edge, or on a module's edge on the overview, stands over the chain its lines run down, keeping its order and gaps. An edge whose chips wrap or fold stays packed.

## Tracing

A reader answers "what does X use" by following X's lines. Rules 16 to 23 measure that walk. The checker builds the ink graph of a drawing: straight pieces of one colour, which join where one ends on another or where a dot sits, and which only cross otherwise. A reader follows a vertical piece the way it was drawn and a horizontal piece either way, since a bus's track carries lines both ways. A traced relation is the path through that graph, with the fewest bends, from where its line leaves its start to an arrowhead on its end. The relations the checker expects are the pairs of places the view requests before routing, so a relation the routing drops is lost. A relation whose end has no shape on the drawing is lost too. A chip that stands for several frame ports carries every port's id, and ink to it traces each of their relations. The expected pairs are still the renderer's own requests: a stored relation the view never asks for is not caught by this check.

16. **Few bends on a traced path.** Target: at most 4 per relation, and a median of at most 2 per view. One crossing costs a reader about as much as 38 degrees of turning (Ware et al. 2002). Measured, not yet enforced. A line that spans rows runs straight down its chain, so it turns only where it leaves its start's track and where it reaches its end's. A line that steps from one bus to another turns twice more at the step.
17. **A dot at every split and merge.** Where ink of one colour goes three or more ways, the renderer draws a dot in the line's colour. Where a line crosses onto a trunk that runs on past it, the dot is the only mark that says they join. A dot counts only for ink of its own colour: it neither joins nor marks lines of another. Every split counts on its own, however close it sits to another or to a box. Enforced: 0 points without a dot.
18. **No phantom paths.** The ink joins two places only when a relation does. An arrival bus is shared only by lines from exactly the same places. Only one departure track meets an arrival trunk, at its top, and every other line steps between buses where no reader can follow it the wrong way (rule 4). Measured: every view's phantom pairs are in the metrics table, 71 across the fixtures, down from 191. pstack's overview holds 52 of them: two modules that use each other are joined by one line that reads both ways. Most of the rest are in Orca's and pstack's dense insides. The 215 focus views hold none, since a twin keeps every line one way (Focus views).
19. **Few crossings on a traced path.** Target: at most 3 per relation, and a median of at most 1. Measured, not yet enforced. The rows are ordered on whole paths, chains included. Splitting buses for rule 18 still adds lines, and so crossings, in the densest views.
20. **Long lines get their own lanes.** Every line that passes a row has a dummy there, a slot of its own in the row's order, instead of a corridor beside the row. The beside share measures how much ink runs alongside a row of boxes, with a target under 15%. It counts a straight chain through a row as much as a corridor, so it did not fall with the dummies: 0.088 to 0.111 on this repository's overview, and 0.163 to 0.119 on pstack's.
21. **Boxes keep their size.** The tallest box is at most 1.5 times the median box's height. On the overview a chip stands for the contracts one drawn line carries. It is named when it is one contract and counted (`계약 N개`) when it is more, with the names in its tooltip. The contracts no line in the view reaches fold into one `+N 계약` chip. A box may be wide so the lines below it drop straight. Enforced.
22. **A count belongs to one line.** Each `×N` sits within 10px of the stretch of ink it counts, and at least 5px closer to it than to any other. A gap that holds a bus's stub or drop keeps 16px between the box and the nearest track, so the count fits beside it. A count never sits on a box or on a frame's chip. Measured: 13 of 468 counts across the fixtures still sit between two stretches.
23. **Every view has a title and a key.** Target for a later round.

`eval/render-r1/reference-self.svg` is this repository's overview laid out by hand, the way a person would in draw.io, with the same boxes and relations. `TestReferenceRanksFirst` fails when a measure rates the renderer above it, which would mean the measure counts the wrong thing. That is how rule 21 came to bound height and not area: the hand-laid CLI box is wide so its lines drop straight.

## Flows

A layered view answers "what uses what". A flow answers one question about the order things happen in, such as what `abbd sync` does. It is the numbered figure of the per-module docs agents write: one question, a caption, and steps in execution order, each on the unit whose code runs it, with what the step does, a note (a cache, a TTL, a skip rule), how it fails, and the units it calls.

- **Stored as facts with evidence.** A flow is `.abbd/flows/<key>.md`, written by `abbd flow KEY FILE`. A reader's is INFERRED, and a human's, with `--source`, is USER_STATED. Every step cites the code it rests on, each quote held once in its file, and is stamped with the lines around its quotes, so an edit there stales that step alone. A flow stores no unit and no relation. Its steps name stored units, and a call is drawn only where a live stored relation joins the step's unit, or a unit inside it, to the called unit, so a chip never draws an edge the graph lacks. A step whose unit or call the state lost, or whose code moved, is STALE, and `abbd status --check` fails until the flow is read and stored again.
- **Drawn in HTML.** The figure is a numbered list, not an SVG. The browser wraps every line, so no label is cut. The steps read top to bottom in their order, and the only lines are the arrows from each step to the next: no crossings, no bends. Consecutive steps on one unit share one cell beside them, which groups by the stored unit only. At 560px and below the cell stands above its steps.
- **Linked into the tree.** A step's unit opens its own inside, or else the inside of the nearest unit that holds it. A call opens the same way, and a contract with neither opens the inside of the module that shows its notes. The overview lists every flow by its question, and each module's inside lists the flows with steps on the units it holds, by step number.

## What the overview draws

Every view is deterministic, so the same state renders as identical bytes. Every view, the overview included, draws every live relation whatever its origin. A reader's coupling (판독) carries its hollow dot there too, because sync re-checks its quote on every run. A dead coupling, whose quote is gone, is drawn nowhere. The overview used to leave reader's couplings out, and on Orca (evaluation round 5) that erased every real platform boundary: the phone-to-relay WebSocket, the E2EE handshake, the push gateway and the native helper sockets were all found by readers, mostly on units inside a module.

So a relation from anywhere inside a top-level unit to another top-level unit is drawn on the overview as that unit's own: it crosses the boundary the overview shows. A unit inside a module is never drawn on the overview, and a change that stays inside a module never moves it.

## Grouping levels

A level of the tree is the one the project shows. A merge is stored only where an engineer would draw it: a directory that exists in the repository, a package or workspace, a skill family, a subsystem the project's own docs name, or an abstraction the code itself shows, such as a layer or the implementers of one interface. A merge made to make a view smaller or a dense view easier to draw goes past that, and makes the system harder to understand. The blueprint splits its views into the whole (the overview), a part (a module's inside) and one unit (a focus view), and a click on a box follows that module inside. That is how a crowded or dense level is read. The grouping is stored as units (MOD) and `parent` links, and the renderer never invents one at render time.

- **The extraction or a reader names it.** The extraction script reports the parents the code shows. The supplement step of `abbd-setup` adds only a grouping the project itself shows, as a MOD whose `evidence` quotes the project naming or showing it, and gives each member that part as its `parent`, with `parent_evidence`. `abbd record parent ID PARENT --evidence ...` places a single unit later, and `--source` records a human's statement instead. Such a parent is INFERRED or USER_STATED, and sync keeps it unless the extraction script reports a parent for that unit. A reader's parent lasts only as long as its evidence. A parent that would close a cycle is refused.
- **Sync's directories.** When a crowded level's units were placed by the script or by nobody, `abbd sync` places them in the directories their code lives in, at the shallowest depth that splits them. Inside a module it groups only the units below the module's own directory, the deepest one that holds all its anchors: a group of that directory or one above it would repeat the module, and a unit outside it stays in the module as it is. An anchor counts as a directory, and holds itself, when an anchor lies under it, so a module anchored at `src/core` spans `src/core` and not `src`. It stores each directory as a MOD unit whose anchor is that directory with the symbol `(group)`. It never moves a unit a reader or a human placed. It recomputes them on every sync and reuses IDs by directory.

## Known gaps

Twelve full views of 69, and one focus view of 205, still break a rule. Their current measurements are recorded in `fixtures_test.go` as debt. They are allowed to improve but never to get worse, and the test fails once a view keeps every rule until it is taken off the list.

- **Dense cyclic views.** Orca's mobile app (12 of 12 units in one cycle), desktop main process (13 of 15), the runtime (10 of 13) and the renderer (8 of 9) are import cycles among real siblings. Their climbing lines are dashed, but there are more than rule 13 allows: 11, 10, 7 and 6. The chains took desktop main from 30 phantom pairs to 0 and the runtime from 5 to 1, and cleared the runtime's and the mobile app's crowded channels and close pairs. Desktop main draws more separate lines for that, and one line runs 244px out of its way. Its crossings are down to 87 from 89. pstack's principles inside has 3 back lines. These views are read one box at a time through their focus views. No merge fixes them, because the cycle is not a unit the project shows.
- **Orca's `src/` directories.** The renderer used to fold a 9-unit cycle in `src/shared/` and a 5-unit cycle in the top-level `src/` into virtual boxes. They are drawn in place now. `shared types and schemas` is `src/shared/` itself, so sync makes no directory group inside it: it holds 22 boxes where rule 1 allows 16, 6 of its 27 arriving lines climb where rule 13 allows 5, and one line runs 468px out of its way. The top-level `src/` has one pair of lines closer than 5px for 24px.
- **This repository's insides.** The CLI, `/abbd`, `internal/model` and `internal/topology` views have 5, 5, 3 and 2 climbing lines, more than rule 13 allows. In the CLI, its units use more than fits on the bottom edge, so used ports move to the top and their lines climb.
- **pstack's insides.** `setup and help` runs lines side by side for 2228px, past rule 14's limit of 2200px. Its frame's chips feed each unit from many sides, and the steps between buses add short parallel tracks.
- **Dense overviews.** pstack's twelve skill families use each other in 42 of 66 pairs. The overview keeps every rule, but it is still a busy picture, with 52 phantom pairs: the bundled lines between families that use each other read both ways. Its twelve focus views have none, and neither do Orca's desktop main process's fifteen. The full views keep their phantoms and crossings, and are read through the focus views.
- **Focus views.** Of 205 focus views across the fixtures, one, Orca's `mobile/session`, has a median of 3 bends. The three that had 3 bends now have 1 or 2: a frame's chips were set over where their lines ran before the frame widened, so a port line stepped sideways out of its chip, and now each chip sits over its line. One focus view, pstack's `docs/harness/claude-code.md`, carries debt in `fixtures_test.go`: lines of two kinds between the same boxes run side by side for 708px (rule 14).
- **Secondary edges still rank.** Laying full views out on strong edges only (reader-found lines drawn after placement) was measured and dropped. Orca's desktop main process draws no reader-found line, so it stays at 87 crossings. This repository's overview reaches the hand reference's median of 2 bends and 1 crossing per line that way, but then sends 5 lines up where rule 13 allows 4, and pstack's overview goes from 12 crossings to 25. The numbers are in `eval/render-r4/p12.txt`.
